\section{Methodology}~\label{sec:methodology}
%********************************************************************************

The methodology for generating descriptors is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:semantically-learned-global-descriptor-pipeline}. The first stage preprocesses the point cloud for projection onto the panoramic image.

\begin{figure*}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Semantically_Learned_descriptor_overleaf.png}
	\caption{Proposed pipeline for generating global descriptors from multimodal data. The pipeline integrates camera and LiDAR preprocessing, multimodal fusion, and a semantic--geometry-guided descriptor module. Panoramic RGB images and projected LiDAR point clouds are processed through dedicated branches and fused to generate semantically enriched features. Backprojection and refinement restore the point-domain representation, which is then aggregated into a compact global scene descriptor.}	
	\label{fig:semantically-learned-global-descriptor-pipeline}

Then, based on the model presented by \cite{Lin2025}, the point cloud and the images are fed into the network to obtain perceptual features from both modalities individually through feature fusion using attention-based models.
Next, projected features are transferred to the 3D point domain and processed with sparse convolutions. Finally, a trainable global descriptor transforms the multimodal point features into an embedding for retrieving semantically related candidate scenes.

\subsection{Panoramic Images Generation}~\label{subsec:panoramic-image-generation}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For comprehensive scene understanding and environmental awareness, the use of panoramic images offers significant advantages over single-camera setups, particularly in outdoor and large-scale autonomous perception scenarios. The panoramic representation of our multi-sensor system provides a 180-degree field of view, enabling the system to capture in front of the vehicle spatial context of a scene in a single frame. 

This holistic perception reduces blind spots and allows the model to reason about long-range spatial relationships between objects that would otherwise be distributed across multiple independent camera views. By observing the entire surrounding environment simultaneously, the system gains a more coherent representation of scene layout, object distribution, and structural continuity. 
In contrast, single-camera images are inherently limited by their narrower FoV, which can restrict contextual reasoning and lead to partial observations of large structures such as buildings, intersections, or dynamic objects. 

\begin{figure*}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Stitching images_to_panoramic_uao.pdf} 	
	\caption{Panoramic image generation through multi-camera homography alignment and stitching.}	
	\label{fig:panoramic-image-generation}
\end{figure*}

This limitation may require additional multi-view fusion strategies to approximate a global understanding of the environment. 
Panoramic imagery, therefore, naturally supports richer contextual modeling, improved spatial completeness, and more consistent global representations of complex scenes.

The process begins with feature extraction, where salient keypoints and corresponding binary descriptors are detected using the ORB~\cite{Rublee2011} algorithm. ORB is selected because of its computational efficiency and invariance to in-plane rotation. The input images have a spatial resolution of $1280 \times 720$ pixels.
Following feature extraction, a keypoint matching stage is performed between overlapping image pairs. Descriptor correspondences are established using a nearest-neighbor search strategy, typically based on Hamming distance due to ORB’s binary descriptors. These correspondences provide the geometric constraints necessary for estimating the transformation between views. \\

Next, a homography matrix is computed using the matched keypoints. To ensure robustness against incorrect correspondences (outliers), the homography is estimated using the RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus) algorithm. RANSAC iteratively selects minimal subsets of correspondences to compute candidate homographies and retains the solution that maximizes inlier consistency, thereby improving geometric accuracy.
Once the homography matrix is obtained, a perspective warping step is applied. One image is projected onto the coordinate frame of the reference image using a perspective transformation, aligning the overlapping regions into a common plane. 
This warping compensates for viewpoint differences between cameras under the planar scene assumption. Finally, after geometric alignment and stitching, the resulting composite image is cropped to remove redundant regions and boundary artifacts. The final panoramic output has a spatial resolution of $908 \times 230$ pixels. This process is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:panoramic-image-generation}.

\subsection{Preprocessing of the Point Cloud}~\label{subsec:lidar-preprocessing}
%-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Several strategies can be employed to project 3D point clouds onto the image domain, including perspective, cylindrical~\cite{Kang2019RobustCP}, and spherical projections~\cite{HeYingshen2020}. The selection of the projection model is strongly influenced by the imaging geometry, sensor configuration, and especially the field of view of the camera system. 

In scenarios involving panoramic images with a wide angular coverage, spherical projection offers a more consistent geometric formulation than conventional perspective projection. Unlike perspective models, which are inherently limited to narrower fields of view and may introduce severe distortions at large viewing angles, spherical parameterization maintains the angular continuity of the scene by directly mapping LiDAR points according to their azimuth and elevation angles. This property ensures a more uniform spatial correspondence between 3D points and image pixels, thereby improving the stability of cross-modal alignment and facilitating more reliable multimodal feature fusion. \\

\textbf{Formulation of perspective projection}. Let $\left\{\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{S} \right\}$ denote a training sample, where $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 5}$ is a LiDAR point cloud and $N$ is the number of points. Each point $\mathbf{P}_i$ contains 3D coordinates $(x,y,z)$ and two sensor attributes $(r,i)$. Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ be an RGB image, where $H$ and $W$ are its height and width, and let $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ contain the point-wise semantic labels. We project $\mathbf{P}$ from the LiDAR coordinate system onto the panoramic image to obtain 2D LiDAR features $\mathbf{\tilde{X}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C}$, where $C$ is the number of projected channels.
Perspective projection associates LiDAR measurements with image information. Let a 3D point be represented in the LiDAR coordinate system as $\mathbf{P}_L = [\mathbf{X}_L, \mathbf{Y}_L, \mathbf{Z}_L, 1]^T$. Before projection, the point is transformed into the camera coordinate system using the LiDAR--camera extrinsic calibration~\cite{verma2019automaticextrinsiccalibrationcamera, Cai-Yujian2021, Gerardo2011, Geiger2012AutomaticCA, Jing2022, Zang2022}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Spherical_point_cloud_projection_v2.pdf} 	
	\caption{Perspective projection of the LiDAR point cloud onto the panoramic image plane.}	
	\label{fig:spherical-projection}
\end{figure*}

This rigid-body transformation comprises a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ and a translation vector $\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 1}$. The point in the camera coordinate frame is

\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{P}_C = \begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{X}_C \\
		\mathbf{Y}_C \\
		\mathbf{Z}_C 
	\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{R} \begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{X}_L \\
		\mathbf{Y}_L \\
		\mathbf{Z}_L \\
	\end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{t}
\end{equation}
Once expressed in the camera coordinate system, the point is projected onto the image plane using the pinhole camera model. The normalized coordinates are mapped to pixel coordinates using the intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix}
		f_x & 0   & c_x \\
		0   & f_y & c_y \\
		0   &   0 &  1
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $f_x$ and $f_y$ denote the focal lengths in pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the camera principal point. The final pixel location $(u,v)$ is
\begin{equation}
	u = f_x \frac{\mathbf{X}_C}{\mathbf{Z}_C} + c_x \quad  v = f_y \frac{\mathbf{Y}_C}{\mathbf{Z}_C} + c_y 
\end{equation}
Combining the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters gives
\begin{equation}
	\lambda \begin{bmatrix}
		u \\ v \\ 1
	\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{K}[\mathbf{R} | \mathbf{t}] \begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{X}_L \\
		\mathbf{Y}_L \\
		\mathbf{Z}_L \\
		1
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = Z_C$ is the projective scale factor. This procedure is repeated for each camera. The stitching method described in Section~\ref{subsec:panoramic-image-generation} then constructs the panoramic image and its point-cloud projection.

\subsection{Deep Multimodal Semantic Segmentation}~\label{subsec:multi-modal-semantic-segmentation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To construct a semantically enriched point-cloud representation, we adopt a dual-branch encoder--decoder architecture. This design enables the simultaneous extraction of low-level structural details and high-level contextual features from the two sensing modalities.
The upper branch is built upon the ResNet34 backbone \cite{he2015deepresiduallearningimage}, responsible for extracting rich visual appearance features from RGB images. In parallel, the lower branch is based on the SalsaNext architecture \cite{cortinhal2024salsanextfastuncertaintyawaresemantic}, specifically designed to process LiDAR data and extract geometric and structural features from the projected point cloud representation.
Following the VPA-Net framework \cite{Lin2025}, feature integration is performed at an intermediate stage using the Feature Fusion Attention Module (FFAM). This module facilitates adaptive cross-modal interaction by learning attention weights that emphasize complementary information between the visual and geometric modalities, thereby enhancing the consistency and discriminative power of the fused representation. \\

During preprocessing, a panoramic image is generated and the point cloud is projected onto the corresponding mosaic representation. Ensuring that both modalities share identical spatial dimensions is critical to guarantee alignment compatibility within the network architecture and to enable effective feature-level fusion.

\textbf{Formulation of the two-branch network}. Let $T$ and $\tilde{T}$ denote the camera and LiDAR branches, respectively. Let $\mathbf{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times S}$ and $\mathbf{\tilde{O}} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times S}$ be their output probabilities, where $S$ is the number of semantic classes. The branch outputs are

\begin{equation}
	\begin{cases}
		\mathbf{O} = T(\mathbf{X}), \\
	    \mathbf{\tilde{O}} = \tilde{T}(\mathbf{\tilde{X}})
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Design of the Feature Fusion Attention++ Module}. Inspired by \cite{Lin2025}, we propose FFAM++, depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:ffam_pp}. It extends the original FFAM with a hierarchical dual-attention mechanism. Camera and LiDAR features are first concatenated and projected through a convolutional fusion layer. A spatial attention map then conditions the LiDAR response on the fused multimodal context. FFAM++ additionally incorporates global channel-wise attention based on adaptive average pooling, allowing the network to reweight feature channels using scene-level context. The final output is obtained by residual addition of the original LiDAR features and the refined attention response. The experiments evaluate this complete multimodal configuration; they do not isolate the effect of the added channel gate. \\

\textbf{Formulation of FFAM++}. Let $F_c \in \mathbb{R}^{C_c \times H \times W}$ and $F_l \in \mathbb{R}^{C_l \times H \times W}$ be camera and LiDAR feature maps, respectively. The fused representation is obtained by channel-wise concatenation followed by a $3\times3$ convolution, LeakyReLU, and batch normalization, denoted by $\phi$:

\begin{equation}
	F_f = \phi ([F_l, F_c])
	\label{eq:concatenation-image-lidar}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	F_s = \mathcal{A}_{s}(F_f),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_{s}$ is the spatial fusion-attention block inherited from FFAM. FFAM++ then obtains a global channel descriptor and transforms it through a bottleneck with reduction ratio $r=16$:
\begin{equation}
	z = \mathrm{AAP}(F_s),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	A_c = \sigma\!\left(W_2\,\delta(W_1 z)\right),
\end{equation}
where $W_1$ and $W_2$ are learnable $1\times1$ convolutions, $\delta$ is ReLU, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $\mathrm{AAP}$ denotes adaptive average pooling. The channel-refined response and residual output are
\begin{equation}
	F_r = F_s \otimes A_c,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	F_{out} = F_l + F_r.
\end{equation}
Here, $\otimes$ denotes channel-wise broadcast multiplication. This formulation matches the implemented hierarchy: convolutional multimodal fusion, spatial attention, global channel gating, and residual addition. Because the current ablation introduces RGB and FFAM++ simultaneously, the experiments characterize the complete multimodal configuration but do not isolate FFAM++ from the original FFAM.

\subsection{Refinement module}~\label{subsec:refinement-mod}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Transferring fused LiDAR features back to the 3D domain before descriptor construction helps restore geometric consistency. Multimodal fusion is performed in a projected 2D space, where discretization, occlusion, and feature-resolution reduction can remove spatial detail. Backprojecting the fused features to metric 3D coordinates restores their association with the physical scene structure and supports point-domain refinement. \\

We propose a refinement module that transforms fused range-view (RV) feature maps into a geometrically consistent semantic 3D representation. The pipeline consists of four stages: backprojection, sparse tensor construction, sparse 3D convolutional refinement, and voxel-wise semantic classification, as depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:refinement-mod}. \\
First, the multimodal encoder–decoder network produces a dense RV feature map. Using the known projection indices of each LiDAR point, features are lifted from the 2D RV domain back to their corresponding 3D points through direct indexing. This yields point-wise semantic features defined in Euclidean space.
Next, the 3D space is discretized into voxels with a predefined resolution. Points are assigned to voxels based on their spatial coordinates, and features within the same voxel are aggregated. Only occupied voxels are retained, forming a sparse tensor representation. This avoids unnecessary computation over empty space and enables efficient large-scale processing. \\

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/Refinement_Module_overleaf.png}
	\caption{Semantic Map Refinement via Back-Projection and Sparse 3D Convolutions. Overview of the proposed refinement module. Map features are first back-projected into a 3D representation and initialized as sparse voxel features. These features are then processed by sparse 3D convolutions followed by a semantic classification layer to generate a refined semantic point cloud}	
	\label{fig:refinement-mod}
\end{figure}

The sparse voxel tensor is then refined using stacked sparse 3D convolution layers \cite{graham20173dsemanticsegmentationsubmanifold, BaoyuanLiu2015}. For each active voxel, features are updated by aggregating information from neighboring active voxels within a local kernel. This operation propagates contextual information in true 3D space, enforcing spatial consistency while preserving computational efficiency.
Finally, a voxel-wise linear classifier produces semantic logits, which are converted into class probabilities via softmax function. 
The predicted voxel labels can be directly interpreted as a semantic 3D map or projected back to individual points to obtain a semantic point cloud.

\subsection{Semantic-Guided Multimodal Descriptor module (SGMD)}~\label{subsec:Semantic-descriptor-network}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fig.~\ref{fig:semantic-descriptor-mod} shows the SGMD architecture. The refined semantic point representation is processed by linear projection, semantic-guided attention, and latent alignment to generate a scene descriptor. \\


\begin{figure*}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Semantic_descriptor_learning_module_overleaf.png}
	\caption{Semantic-guided global descriptor learning module. Refined geometric features, semantic embeddings, depth information, and semantic confidence are jointly aggregated through attention to generate a compact global scene descriptor.}	
	\label{fig:semantic-descriptor-mod}
\end{figure*}

Given $N$ refined point features $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{C}$, depths $d_i\in\mathbb{R}$, and semantic probability vectors $\mathbf{p}_i\in[0,1]^S$, the module learns a mapping
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{F}_{\theta} :
	\left(\mathbb{R}^{N\times C},\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1},
	[0,1]^{N\times S}\right)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}.
\end{equation}
The implemented head uses a hidden dimension of 128 and produces a 256-dimensional descriptor. To bound memory use, at most 8192 points are sampled from each observation. Local features are first refined with a shared point-wise convolution, batch normalization, and ReLU:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{h}_i =
	\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathrm{BN}\!\left(W_h\mathbf{x}_i\right)\right).
\end{equation}
The semantic embedding is computed from log probabilities:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{e}_i = W_s\log(\mathbf{p}_i+\epsilon).
\end{equation}
Feature, depth, and semantic information are concatenated and passed through an attention MLP:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{u}_i = [\mathbf{h}_i,d_i,\mathbf{e}_i],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	a_i = \mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{u}_i).
\end{equation}
Semantic entropy and confidence are defined as
\begin{equation}
	H_i = -\sum_{s=1}^{S}p_{i,s}\log(p_{i,s}+\epsilon),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	c_i = 1-\frac{H_i}{\log S}.
\end{equation}
Following the implementation, confidence modulates the attention logit with a residual floor:
\begin{equation}
	\tilde{a}_i = a_i(0.5+c_i).
\end{equation}
The normalized point weights and global feature are
\begin{equation}
	\alpha_i =
	\frac{\exp(\tilde{a}_i)}
	{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\tilde{a}_j)},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{g} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_i\mathbf{h}_i.
\end{equation}
Finally, the feature is projected and L2-normalized:
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{d}=W_d\mathbf{g},
	\qquad
	\mathbf{f}=\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{d}\|_2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Landmark-Aware Candidate Re-ranking}~\label{subsec:landmark-reranking}

In addition to direct SGMD retrieval, we evaluate an optional landmark-aware re-ranking strategy that does not modify or retrain the descriptor network. SGMD first retrieves a pool of 200 candidates using cosine similarity. For each query and candidate, a semantic--spatial signature is then constructed from the predicted semantic evidence and local LiDAR coordinates. The signature retains six persistent anchor classes: building, fence, vegetation, trunk, pole, and traffic-sign. For each class, it encodes semantic mass, centroid, radial dispersion, angular distribution, and radial distribution.

The descriptor-induced order and the landmark-signature order are combined through normalized rank consensus. If $r_d(c)$ and $r_l(c)$ denote the normalized descriptor and landmark ranks of candidate $c$, respectively, the final ordering score is
\begin{equation}
	r_{\mathrm{con}}(c)
	=
	(1-\alpha)r_d(c)+\alpha r_l(c),
\end{equation}
where lower scores are ranked first and $\alpha=0.40$ in the reported experiments. This variant is denoted by SGMD + landmark. It is a lightweight post-retrieval ordering mechanism included in the present work and requires no additional trainable module.

\subsection{Loss Functions}~\label{subsec:loss-function}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

The framework uses separate objectives for semantic segmentation/refinement and descriptor learning. Because LiDAR semantic classes are highly imbalanced, the segmentation objective combines class-sensitive and region-based losses. \\

For the segmentation task, we employ a combination of multiclass focal loss~\cite{LinTsung2020} and Lovász--Softmax loss~\cite{BermanMaxim2018}. Focal loss addresses class imbalance by dynamically down-weighting well-classified samples and emphasizing hard or misclassified examples. This mechanism focuses the objective on rare or difficult classes, which are often underrepresented in large-scale outdoor datasets.

However, optimizing only the classification loss does not necessarily lead to improvements in region-based evaluation metrics. \\

To address this limitation, we incorporate the Lovász--Softmax loss, which provides a differentiable surrogate for optimizing the intersection-over-union (IoU) metric. Combining focal and Lovász--Softmax losses balances class-level discrimination and region-level accuracy in class-imbalanced environments.

Let $S \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ contain the labels projected into camera coordinates. Pixels without a projected label are initialized to zero.
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{S}_{h,w} := \mathbf{y}_i
\end{equation}
The multiclass focal loss for the LiDAR stream is

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{foc} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\alpha_s \mathcal{I}\left \{ \mathbf{S}_{h,w} = s \right \} FL(\mathbf{\tilde{O}}_{s,h,w})
\end{equation}

where $FL(p) = -(1-p)^2\log(p)$ is the focal-loss function and $\alpha_s$ is the weight of class $s$. Moreover, $K = \sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W} \mathcal{I}\left\{\mathbf{S}_{h,w}=s\right\}$ is the number of available labels, and $\mathcal{I}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function. The multiclass focal loss for the camera stream is
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{foc} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{w=1}^{W}\alpha_s \mathcal{I}\left \{ \mathbf{S}_{h,w} = s \right \} FL(\mathbf{O}_{s,h,w})
\end{equation}

The Lovász--Softmax loss for the LiDAR stream is

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{lov} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\overline{\Delta_{J_s}}(\mathbf{\tilde{m}}(s))
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{\tilde{m}}_i(s) = \begin{cases}
		1 - \mathbf{\tilde{O}}_{s,h,w}  & \text{if} \; s = \mathbf{S}_{h,w}, \\
		\mathbf{\tilde{O}}_{s,h,w} & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases} 
\end{equation}
$\overline{\Delta_{J_s}}$ denotes the Jaccard-loss extension for class $s$, and $\mathbf{\tilde{m}}(s) \in [0,1]^N$ is the corresponding error vector. The Lovász--Softmax loss for the camera stream is

\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{lov} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\overline{\Delta_{J_s}}(\mathbf{m}(s))
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{m}_i(s) = \begin{cases}
		1 - \mathbf{O}_{s,h,w}  & \text{if} \; s = \mathbf{S}_{h,w}, \\
		\mathbf{O}_{s,h,w} & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases} 
\end{equation}

Accordingly, the fused LiDAR-stream and camera-stream segmentation objectives are
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fus}}
	=
	\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\mathrm{lov}}
	+\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\mathrm{foc}},
	\qquad
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cam}}
	=
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lov}}
	+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{foc}}.
\end{equation}

For descriptor learning, two views are constructed from the same refined observation. The first view uses the sampled refined features directly, while the second adds zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.01$ to those features. Their L2-normalized descriptors form the positive pair. Other observations in the same mini-batch act as negatives. For a mini-batch of size $B$, the implemented InfoNCE objective is
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}}
	=
	-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{k=1}^{B}
	\log
	\frac{
	\exp\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{k}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}^{k}_{j}/\tau\right)}
	{\sum_{l=1}^{B}
	\exp\!\left(\mathbf{z}^{k}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{z}^{l}_{j}/\tau\right)},
	\label{eq:contrastive-loss}
\end{equation}
where $\tau=0.07$. This denominator compares each anchor with every descriptor in the opposite view and therefore yields the $B\times B$ similarity matrix used by the implementation.

The reported checkpoints use semantic probabilities inside the SGMD attention mechanism, but do not activate an additional semantic-distribution loss in the contrastive-loss call. This distinction is important: semantic guidance is architectural in the evaluated model, whereas descriptor discrimination is optimized through Eq.~\ref{eq:contrastive-loss}. The point-level refinement objective is
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ref}}
	=
	\mathcal{L}^{3D}_{\mathrm{lov}}
	+0.1\,\mathcal{L}^{3D}_{\mathrm{foc}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}^{3D}_{\mathrm{lov}}$ and $\mathcal{L}^{3D}_{\mathrm{foc}}$ denote the Lov\'asz--Softmax and focal losses computed from the native point-level refinement logits, respectively. The active objective changes with the training phase:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{(p)}_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\begin{cases}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fus}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cam}},
& p=1,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cam}},
& p=2,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fus}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ref}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cam}},
& p=3,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}},
& p=4,\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fus}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ref}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cam}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}},
& p=5,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:phase-dependent-total-loss}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\mathrm{cam}}=0.3$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}=0.1$. $p$ corresponds to each phase. Thus, the camera focal and Lov\'asz losses are active in phases 1--3 and in final joint fine-tuning (phase 5). They are omitted in phase 4 because the camera/fusion branches are frozen while the refinement and descriptor modules are optimized.
